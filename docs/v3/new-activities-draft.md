## 2. Rules these drafts follow

- **Same shape as a v2 step:** an introduction, one prompt, three guided questions with tap-to-start options, a wrap-up. They'd run on the existing writing screen, so "Need to talk?", the support card and the gentle check apply unchanged.
- **No scores, ratings, labels or rankings.** Nothing asks a student to rate themselves.
- **No streaks or counts.** Where an activity says "try it this week", it's once, with one reflection.
- **Everyday situations only.** No question asks about risk, harm or anything clinical.
- **Each stands alone.** None needs another activity done first, so the levels stay a map (D3).
- **No stories or media attached.** Only a person adds those.
- **Research stays out of the student wording.** The evidence is in section 4, with what I could and couldn't confirm.

## 3. The drafts

### 1. The If-Then Plan (Level 3, self-regulation)

**Introduction.** Everyone has moments where they react before they've decided to. Trying harder in the moment mostly doesn't work, because by then it's already happening. What works better is deciding earlier. You pick the moment in advance and choose your move while you're calm.

**Prompt.** Pick one moment that keeps catching you out, and write the plan for next time.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| What's a moment where you react faster than you'd like? Keep it ordinary. | When my brother or sister winds me up · When I get a message that annoys me · When I'm losing at something · When someone corrects me in front of people · When I'm told to get off my phone |
| What usually happens, and what would you rather do instead? | I snap, and I'd rather pause · I fire back a message, and I'd rather wait · I give up, and I'd rather take a minute · I go quiet and stew, and I'd rather say one calm thing |
| Write it as one line: "If ___, then I will ___." Make the "then" small enough to do in five seconds. | If I feel my face go hot, then I'll take three slow breaths · If I want to reply straight away, then I'll put the phone face down first · If I'm about to walk off, then I'll say "give me a sec" |

**Wrap-up.** You've made the decision ahead of time, so you don't have to make it in the heat of the moment. It won't work every time. When it doesn't, that's information about the plan, not about you. Change the "then" and go again.

### 2. Make It Easier (Level 3, self-discipline)

**Introduction.** People who look disciplined usually aren't fighting harder than you. They've set things up so there's less to fight. The phone's in another room. The bag's packed the night before. This activity is about changing the set-up, not gritting your teeth.

**Prompt.** Pick one thing you want to do more regularly, and redesign what's around it.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| What's one thing you keep meaning to do and keep putting off? | Homework before dinner · Training or exercise · Practising an instrument · Getting to bed earlier · Reading |
| What gets in the way, right at the moment you'd start? | My phone is right there · I can't find what I need · I'm comfortable where I am · I don't know where to begin · Someone else is around and distracting |
| What's one change to the set-up that would make starting easier? | Leave my phone in another room · Put what I need out the night before · Do it in a different spot · Decide the first two minutes in advance · Do it alongside someone |

**Try it.** Make the change once this week. Come back and write one line about what was different.

**Wrap-up.** You didn't need more willpower. You needed a better set-up. That's a skill you can use on anything.

### 3. The Proof File (Level 4, self-efficacy)

**Introduction.** Believing you can do something hard mostly comes from one place: having done hard things before. The trouble is you forget. Once something's easy, you stop counting it. This activity collects the proof.

**Prompt.** Name three things that used to be hard for you and aren't now, and how that happened.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| What's something you can do now that you couldn't do a few years ago? Big or small. | Swim properly · Speak up in class · Cook a meal · Catch public transport on my own · Play a song all the way through · Keep going in a subject I found hard |
| How did you get from not being able to, to being able to? | I did it badly lots of times first · Someone showed me · I watched how someone else did it · I broke it into smaller bits · I kept turning up |
| What's something in front of you now that feels the way that used to feel? | A subject · A sport or skill · A conversation I need to have · Something I've signed up for · Something I haven't started yet |

**Wrap-up.** That's your proof file. Next time something feels impossible, the question isn't "can I do it?" It's "what did I do last time something felt like this?"

### 4. The Smallest Version (Level 4, self-confidence)

**Introduction.** Most people wait to feel confident before they have a go. It works the other way round. You have a go, it goes okay, and the feeling turns up afterwards. The trick is making the first go small enough that you'll actually do it.

**Prompt.** Pick one thing you've been waiting to feel ready for, and shrink it.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| What's something you'd like to do but keep waiting to feel ready for? | Trying out for something · Answering a question in class · Starting a conversation with someone new · Posting or showing something I made · Asking for help |
| What's the smallest version of it, one you could do this week? | Turn up and watch one session · Answer one question I'm sure of · Say hi and one more sentence · Show it to one person I trust · Ask one question |
| When and where will you do it? | Tomorrow at school · At training this week · On the weekend · Next time I see them |

**Try it.** Do the small version once. Come back and write what happened, in a sentence or two.

**Wrap-up.** However it went, you did the thing that builds confidence: you had a go before you felt ready. The next version can be a bit bigger, when you choose.

### 5. Backing My Call (Level 4, self-trust)

**Introduction.** Trusting yourself doesn't mean always being right, and it doesn't mean never asking anyone. It means knowing your own judgement well enough to tell when to back it and when to get a second opinion.

**Prompt.** Look at one time you backed your own call, and one time you didn't.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| Think of a time you went with your own judgement, even though others saw it differently. What happened? | I chose a subject or activity others didn't · I said no to something · I stuck with a friend · I did the work my own way · I changed my mind about something |
| Think of a time you went against your own judgement. What made you do that? | I didn't want to stand out · Someone seemed surer than me · It was easier · I didn't want an argument · I wasn't sure I knew enough |
| Looking at both, when is your judgement worth backing, and when is it worth checking with someone? | I'm usually right about people · I'm usually right about what I can handle · I should check when I'm angry or rushed · I should check when it's something new to me |

**Wrap-up.** You know a bit more about when to trust your own read. Asking someone else isn't the opposite of self-trust. Knowing when to ask is part of it.

### 6. The Part I Choose (Level 5, autonomy)

**Introduction.** A lot of your week is decided by other people: school, family, timetables. That's normal at your age. But inside almost every "have to" there's a part you can choose. Finding that part is what makes something yours.

**Prompt.** Take one "have to" from your week and find what you can choose inside it.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| What's one thing in your week you have to do and didn't choose? | A subject I didn't pick · Jobs at home · A sport or activity someone signed me up for · An assignment · A family thing |
| You may not choose whether. What could you choose about how, when, where or who with? | Which part I start with · When in the day I do it · Where I do it · Who I do it with · How I go about it |
| Is there a reason to do it that's actually yours, even a small one? | It gets me closer to something I want · It matters to someone I care about · I like being someone who follows through · I'd rather do it well than drag it out · Honestly, not yet |

**Wrap-up.** You've found your part of it. And if the honest answer was "not yet", that's worth knowing too. It might be something to raise with someone.

### 7. Say What You Need (Level 5, self-advocacy)

**Introduction.** Speaking up for yourself is a skill, not a personality type. It has three parts: knowing what you need, picking the right person, and saying it in a way they can hear. This activity works on something small and everyday. If what's on your mind is bigger than that, the "Need to talk?" button is always there.

**Prompt.** Pick one small thing you need and haven't asked for, and plan how you'd ask.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| What's one small thing that would make school, home or a friendship work better for you? | More time on something · A clearer explanation · A quieter place to work · To be asked before plans are made · A turn at something |
| Who's the right person to ask? | A teacher · A parent or carer · A coach · A friend · I'm not sure yet |
| Try it in three parts: "I've noticed ___. I need ___. Could we ___?" | I've noticed I fall behind when it moves fast. I need a bit more time. Could we go over it at lunch? · I've noticed I find out about plans last. I need a heads-up. Could we sort it earlier? |

**Try it.** If it feels right, ask once this week. Come back and write one line about how it went. If it doesn't feel right yet, that's your call.

**Wrap-up.** You've worked out what you need, who to ask and how to say it. Asking doesn't guarantee a yes. It does mean the other person knows, and that's where things start to change.

### 8. Mine or Borrowed? (Level 5, self-authorship)

**Introduction.** Most of what you believe arrived before you could choose it: from family, friends, school, your feed. That isn't a problem, and it doesn't mean those beliefs are wrong. This activity just takes a look. You might keep every one of them. The difference is that afterwards you'll have checked.

**Prompt.** Pick a belief you hold and work out where it came from.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| Name one thing you believe about how to live, how to treat people, or what matters. | Family comes first · Hard work pays off · You should always be honest · It's important to stand out · It's important not to make a fuss |
| Where do you think it came from? | My parents or carers · My friends · My culture or faith · Things I watch or follow · Something that happened to me · I'm not sure |
| Now that you've looked at it: keep it, change it, or still deciding? | Keep it, it's mine now · Keep most of it · I'd change part of it · Still deciding, and that's fine |

**Wrap-up.** Whatever you chose, the belief is a bit more yours than it was ten minutes ago. Working out what you think is a long job. Most adults are still doing it.

## 4. The evidence, and what I couldn't confirm

Checked on 13 Nov 2026. "Confirmed" means I found the publication and its main finding. It doesn't mean any of these activities has itself been tested.

| Activity | Rests on | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The If-Then Plan | If-then plans ("implementation intentions"). Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38, 69–119: across 94 tests, a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals. | **Confirmed.** A 2024 update by Sheeran and colleagues is reported to find a smaller effect on behaviour; I saw that only second-hand. Used with school children in Duckworth, Kirby, Gollwitzer & Oettingen (2013), *Social Psychological and Personality Science* 4(6); citation confirmed, results not read. |
| 2. Make It Easier | Changing the situation beats resisting in the moment. Duckworth, Gendler & Gross (2016), *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11(1), 35–55. | **Confirmed** as the paper's argument. It's a theory paper, not a trial. |
| 3. The Proof File | Past success is the strongest source of self-efficacy. Bandura (1977); Usher & Pajares (2008), *Review of Educational Research* 78, 751–796, a review of school studies. | **Confirmed** from the abstract and later citations. The review notes the strength varies by student and subject. |
| 4. The Smallest Version | The same finding as 3: small real successes build belief. | **Confirmed** for the principle. No source for this exact exercise. |
| 5. Backing My Call | Nothing specific. | **No evidence base found.** "Self-trust" isn't an established research term the way self-efficacy is. This activity is my own design from the master file's description. |
| 6. The Part I Choose | Choice supports motivation. Ryan & Deci (2000); Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008), already checked for D35. | **Confirmed** (see D35). |
| 7. Say What You Need | Self-advocacy as knowledge of self, knowledge of rights, communication and leadership. Test, Fowler, Wood, Brewer & Eddy (2005), *Remedial and Special Education* 26(1), 43–54. | **Confirmed, with a limit.** The framework was built from studies of students with disabilities. Using it for all students is an extension. |
| 8. Mine or Borrowed? | Self-authorship. Baxter Magolda (2001). | **Confirmed, with a real limit.** Her study followed university students into adulthood, and most reached self-authorship in their late twenties and thirties. Evidence in school-age students is thin. The draft treats this as a first look, not something a teenager is expected to have done. |

## 5. Things for David to decide

1. **The wording,** line by line. It's a first draft in the v2 voice.
2. **Keeping 7 and 8 from the AI.** "What I need at home" and "what I believe" can be personal. I've proposed they're private, like *Parts of Who You Are*. That means no AI follow-up on them.
3. **Say What You Need and disclosure.** Asking a student what they need can bring up something serious. The draft keeps it small and points to "Need to talk?". This is the same question as sharing with a teacher, which is still open before Phase 2.
4. **Self-authorship at Level 5.** Given the evidence, is a teenager's level the right home for it, or should the wording across the app be softer about it?
5. **Backing My Call.** Keep it with no research behind it, or replace it?
6. **Stories.** None attached. Each self already has a draft story in the master file that could sit before its activity, once a person has verified it.
7. **Still without an activity of their own:** self-esteem and self-compassion (Level 2), actual self and ought self (Level 6). Say if you want drafts for those too. Level 2 wording needs the most care.
