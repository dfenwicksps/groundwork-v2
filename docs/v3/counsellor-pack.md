# Groundwork: wording for a counsellor to read

**Prepared 17 November 2026, for David to send to a school counsellor or psychologist.** About 20 minutes to read. Every word here is what a student would see.

## What Groundwork is

A reflective app for students in Years 7 to 12. Students work through short writing activities about who they are, where they belong, what they care about and the life they want. Their writing stays private: teachers see only whole-class patterns, never a student's words, and never any count below five students.

## What's already in place

- **"Need to talk?"** is on every screen and lists helplines.
- **A crisis check** runs on the student's device as they write. If something reads as risk, a support card with helplines replaces everything else. Nothing is saved, flagged or sent.
- **A gentle check** notices when several recent entries are harsh on the self ("I'm useless") and suggests a different move instead of another lap of the same thoughts. Also on the device only.
- **No scores.** The self-check below gives no number, label or ranking. It suggests a place to start, in words.
- **Kept from the AI:** three of the four activities below are never sent to the AI that asks follow-up questions.

For each item: is anything likely to cause harm, or to bring up more than an everyday activity can hold? Is there wording you'd change? Is anything missing that a student would need? Please mark changes on the page; any change goes back to David before it's used.

## 1. The self-check: six statements about valuing yourself

A student reads each statement and picks one of five answers: Not like me, A little like me, Somewhat like me, Mostly like me, Very like me.

The introduction they see first:

> It isn't a test. There are no right answers, no score and no label. At the end you get a couple of ideas for where to start, in words.
>
> Only you see your answers. Your teacher never does.

The six statements were written to be positive: none asks about being worthless, a burden, unnoticed, or wanting to disappear.

| Self | Statement |
| --- | --- |
| Self-esteem | Most days, I'm okay with being me. |
| Self-worth | Even after a rough week, I still feel like I count. |
| Mattering | There are people who are glad when I turn up. |
| Self-respect | I stick to what matters to me, even when it would be easier to let it go. |
| Self-compassion | When I mess up, I talk to myself the way I'd talk to a friend. |
| Belonging | There's at least one place where I can be myself and still feel part of things. |

**Questions for you:** Would a student who answers "Not like me" to several of these be left feeling worse? Is there a statement you'd reword or drop?

## 2. Belonging: the page a student reads

Each self has a page like this. Belonging is new.

**Belonging.** Having people and places where I fit as I am.

**What it is.** Feeling that you're part of something: a family, a friendship, a team, a class, a community. You're accepted there as you are, and you'd be missed if you weren't there. Most people have it in some places and not in others.

**What it isn't.** Not fitting in. Fitting in is changing yourself to be accepted; belonging is being accepted without having to. And not something you build on your own: it depends on the people around you as well. If you don't feel it somewhere, that doesn't mean something's wrong with you.

**Think about.** Where do you feel most like you can just be yourself? Who's there?

**Try it this week.** Make it a bit easier for someone else to belong this week: save them a seat, ask them along, or use their name.

**Questions for you:** Is "If you don't feel it somewhere, that doesn't mean something's wrong with you" enough for a student who doesn't feel they belong anywhere? Is the try-it fair to ask of a student who is themselves left out?

## 3. Four activities

Each runs on the same screen: the student reads an introduction, then answers three questions one at a time. They can tap a suggested answer or write their own, and can stop at any point. The safety checks above apply.

### 3.1 Not My Results (builds Self-worth)

Sent to the AI for follow-up questions, unless the student or school has turned that off.

**Introduction.** Some weeks go well and some don't. If your sense of being worth something goes up and down with them, every bad result feels like it's about you. This activity looks at the parts of you that don't depend on how the week went.

**Prompt.** Name some things about you that would still be true after your worst week.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| What's something you care about that has nothing to do with marks, wins or likes? | My family · My friends · Being fair · My faith or beliefs · Animals · Making people laugh · Music |
| What's something about how you treat people that stays the same whether you're winning or losing? | I stick up for people · I listen · I turn up · I include people · I keep my word |
| Who is someone who'd think the same of you after a bad week? | A parent or carer · A grandparent · A friend · A pet, honestly · I'm not sure right now |

**Wrap-up.** None of that changed last week, and none of it will change next week. Results matter, and it's fine to care about them. They're just not the bit that makes you count.


*If the last answer was "I'm not sure right now", the wrap-up adds:* That's more common than you'd think, and it can change. Your support circle is a good place to start.

**Questions for you:** Can the questions bring up something heavier than a bad week? Is the extra line for "I'm not sure right now" the right response, and is the support circle the right place to point?

### 3.2 What I'd Say to a Friend (builds Self-compassion)

Kept from the AI.

**Introduction.** When a friend stuffs something up, most of us know what to say. When we do it ourselves, we say things we'd never say to them. This activity borrows the voice you already use for other people.

**Prompt.** Pick a small, ordinary stuff-up from lately, and answer it the way you'd answer a friend.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| What's a small thing you got wrong lately? Keep it everyday. | I forgot something · I said the wrong thing · I did badly at something · I let someone down in a small way · I wasted time I needed |
| What did you say to yourself about it? | "Typical" · "I always do this" · "Everyone saw" · "I'm useless at this" · Not much, I just felt bad |
| If a friend told you they'd done exactly the same thing, what would you say to them? | "Everyone does that" · "It's one time" · "You were tired" · "What do you need now?" · "It's fixable" |

**Wrap-up.** Read back what you'd say to your friend. It's true for you too. Being kind to yourself isn't letting yourself off. It just makes it easier to have another go.

**Questions for you:** Do the examples of what a student says to themselves ("I'm useless at this") risk reinforcing it? Is one activity enough to be useful, or could it feel like being told to cheer up?

### 3.3 Should or Choose? (builds Ought self)

Kept from the AI.

**Introduction.** Everyone carries a list of things they feel they should be: from family, friends, school, their culture, what they see online. Some of those you'd choose anyway. Some you're just carrying. This activity sorts them. Keeping one is as good an answer as dropping one.

**Prompt.** Take a few "I should" sentences and work out which are yours.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| Finish this sentence a few times: "I should…" | be better at school · be more outgoing · be good at sport · look a certain way · know what I want to do · be easier to get along with |
| Pick one. Where does it come from? | My family · My friends · School · Things I see online · My culture or faith · Me · I'm not sure |
| Try saying it as "I choose to…". Does it still sound right? | Yes, I'd choose it anyway · Partly · I'd choose a smaller version · No, it's not really mine · I don't know yet |

**Wrap-up.** Some of your "shoulds" turned out to be yours. Those are easier to carry now. If one of them isn't, you don't have to do anything about it today. It might be worth talking over with someone you trust.

**Questions for you:** Could sorting family or cultural expectations set a student against their family? Does it leave enough room for keeping an expectation?

### 3.4 The Other Version (builds Feared self)

Kept from the AI.

**Introduction.** Most people have a version of themselves they don't want to turn into. Someone who gave up on things, or stopped being kind, or went along with everyone. Thinking about that version isn't a bad sign. It's useful, because it shows you what you care about. It works best when you put the opposite beside it.

**Prompt.** Name one version of you that you'd rather not become, and the version you'd rather be.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| In everyday terms, what's one version of you that you don't want to become? | Someone who gives up when it's hard · Someone who's unkind to get a laugh · Someone who never tries anything · Someone who lets friends down · Someone who just goes along with it |
| What's the opposite version, the one you'd want? | Someone who sticks at things · Someone people feel safe around · Someone who has a go · Someone you can count on · Someone who says what they think |
| What's one small thing this week that the second version would do? | Finish one thing I'd usually drop · Say something kind I'd usually keep in · Try one new thing · Keep one promise · Say what I think once |

**Wrap-up.** The version you don't want just told you what you do want. That's all it's for. One small thing in the right direction counts for more than worrying about the wrong one.

**Questions for you:** Could naming a feared version of themselves be distressing for some students? Are the examples ordinary enough?

## 4. One more, if you have time

**Say What You Need** asks a student to name one small thing they need at school, at home or with a friend, and plan how to ask for it. It keeps to small things and points to "Need to talk?" for anything bigger. David is still deciding what should happen if a student raises something serious here. Your view would help.

**Introduction.** Speaking up for yourself is a skill, not a personality type. It has three parts: knowing what you need, picking the right person, and saying it in a way they can hear. This activity works on something small and everyday. If what's on your mind is bigger than that, the "Need to talk?" button is always there.

**Prompt.** Pick one small thing you need and haven't asked for, and plan how you'd ask.

| Guided question | Options to start from |
| --- | --- |
| What's one small thing that would make school, home or a friendship work better for you? | More time on something · A clearer explanation · A quieter place to work · To be asked before plans are made · A turn at something |
| Who's the right person to ask? | A teacher · A parent or carer · A coach · A friend · I'm not sure yet |
| Try it in three parts: "I've noticed ___. I need ___. Could we ___?" | I've noticed I fall behind when it moves fast. I need a bit more time. Could we go over it at lunch? · I've noticed I find out about plans last. I need a heads-up. Could we sort it earlier? |

**Try it.** If it feels right, ask once this week. Come back and write one line about how it went. If it doesn't feel right yet, that's your call.

**Wrap-up.** You've worked out what you need, who to ask and how to say it. Asking doesn't guarantee a yes. It does mean the other person knows, and that's where things start to change.
